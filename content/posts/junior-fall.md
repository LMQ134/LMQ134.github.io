I feel like I was in a really good studying groove this semester — I mostly just focused on studying. And now that my grades are basically all out, they're clearly a lot higher than in sophomore year. The most important thing is that I fixed my attitude toward studying: for regular classes I'd sit in the front three rows and listen, and it really works! You genuinely learn better that way. At the same time, I noticed that the classmates around me have all started sitting in the front rows too — in a lot of courses you can't even grab a front-row seat anymore. Maybe everyone has caught on by now. Another thing: I started doing a lot more practice problems — for Semiconductor Physics and Digital Circuits I worked through tons of problems, and it genuinely helped raise my GPA.

I had quite a few courses this semester, but most of them were fairly "water" courses (水课 — Chinese college slang for easy, low-effort blow-off classes): just show up, sign the attendance sheet, and finish one paper at the end. There were 4 that actually needed serious study: Semiconductor Physics, Digital Circuits, Semiconductor Optoelectronics, and Applied Stochastic Processes.

### Semiconductor Physics

The professor for this course teaches really well. I didn't watch any other online courses — I just went with what the professor covered in class. The final was a proper, formal exam. I'd say the difficulty was moderate: the term-definition questions were a bit off the beaten path — they tested some obscure corner-case knowledge — but the short-answer questions and calculation problems were fine, and the question types were all pretty much the same as the homework. You do need to memorize a few formulas, like the depletion-layer width and the electric potential — that kind of thing. This course is a bit easier than both Solid State Physics and Quantum Mechanics. The textbook we used was Liu Enke's, and I thought it was well written. I'd suggest buying the companion solutions manual for it — you'll need it for the homework. My advice is to do a few more problems, like the conceptual questions in the solutions, so you understand things better. If you're short on time, for the calculation problems you can just do the ones whose types match the homework.

The overall arc of Semiconductor Physics: first it covers the properties of electrons and holes — mass, velocity, lifetime, and so on. Then it brings in energy bands and energy levels, how carriers are distributed in the bands, and what their concentrations are. Next, you actually calculate the carrier distribution and carrier numbers in a semiconductor at equilibrium — first for a pure semiconductor, then for one containing impurities, and how carrier velocity and conductivity in both depend on the external environment. After that comes carrier motion in non-equilibrium states (driven by concentration differences, electric fields, and temperature) — recombination and generation. And finally, the grand finale: put two different semiconductors together and work out how their carriers behave under equilibrium and non-equilibrium.

### Digital Circuits

The grading in this course is really generous — my coursework grade was a 97, and I scored in the 80s on the final, which made it one of the few major courses where my overall grade topped 90. (All grades here are out of 100.) Besides the coursework grade, this course only has a final, and what's tested is either examples from the textbook or homework problems. In fact, for a lot of chapters the professor tells you which question type will be on the exam — for some, you can even guess how they'll ask it. The last chapter had an asymmetric multivibrator circuit on the exam, and I'd only studied how to do the symmetric one (sob). But thank goodness the professor didn't take off too many points. I feel like the way you study this course isn't much like a course such as Semiconductor Physics — it's more like how you study Advanced Mathematics: do a lot of problems and summarize the question types. Personally, I feel physics is more about understanding, whereas digital circuits aren't hard to understand — the difficulty lies in the problems being tricky. If you have time, you can work through all the end-of-chapter problems in the digital circuits textbook. It looks like a lot, but actually many of them are similar question types grouped together — very regular patterns.

I recommend this online course — it's very detailed and matches the textbook content exactly; it's just that it takes a long time.

### Semiconductor Optoelectronics

This course ran at night, from 7:00 to 9:35 — three periods back to back. Sitting through it was genuinely painful; by the end I just couldn't listen anymore. The professor was actually quite good — it's just that this schedule is absurd. The grading was good too — after all, it's a limited-elective course (限选课 — an elective chosen from a restricted list), and those usually grade a bit more generously — and I got above 90 too, haha. But partly because of that, a lot of people stopped paying attention by the end. Not paying attention is one thing, but if you don't study after class either, then getting above 90 is probably still a bit of a stretch. This course has quizzes during the semester, worth about 20% of the total grade — take them seriously. I'd suggest actually paying attention in this one. What it really covers is the later chapters of Semiconductor Physics — heterojunctions, for example, and turning heterojunctions into lasers. It actually helps with your understanding of semiconductor physics. On the final, 70 points were homework problems, and 30 points were things picked out of the PPT. The professor said at first that it would all be homework problems, so a lot of people just memorized the homework problems and never looked at the PPT — and then it went badly for them. My suggestion: it's fine to not listen in class, but you do need to go study after class. The professor has PPTs — feed the PPT to an AI and have it explain things to you. That's how I studied, and basic understanding was never a problem. Spending a bit more time during the semester beats cramming at the end, by a lot.

### Applied Stochastic Processes

This course went fine; the grading was generous. This was a course I took from the School of Economics, and it turned out the textbook they used was terrible, oh man — seriously, that book was hard going. And on Bilibili, there are basically no online courses on Applied Stochastic Processes worth watching either. Studying it was pure suffering; for the final I leaned entirely on rote-memorizing formulas. The midterm and the final follow the same pattern: first term definitions, then a few big problems picked from the end-of-chapter exercises. This course also ran from 7:00 to 9:35 — I'm speechless. Just pain.

### Microelectronics Fabrication Technology

In a sense, this is a water course — attendance plus one big assignment and you're done. But from a career-development perspective, this course is really, really important. Whether you end up working in a fab or doing design, you'll have to learn this stuff sooner or later. I already kind of regret not listening carefully in it. Right now I'm studying analog integrated circuits and trying to run simulations with Cadence Virtuoso, and I've found that process technology genuinely is crucial — including when you draw layouts, you definitely need to understand the process. My advice: study it seriously.

### Summary

Finals this semester were pretty stressful too. A lot of classmates, facing finals, would talk about "memorize this problem set, memorize that formula" — and I felt the same way. It feels like finals are basically testing recitation; deriving formulas on the spot is obviously unrealistic, so all you can do is memorize — and memorize the solution routines for problems too. Personally, I feel like this isn't quite right, but I can't put my finger on what's wrong with it. Earlier, when I took the Quantum Mechanics exam, it was semi-open-book — you were allowed to bring one A4 sheet — and when it came time to actually do the problems, I found that the formulas I'd written on the sheet weren't much use. It tested real understanding, not who could rote-memorize their way to a high score. That, I think, is what a genuinely good exam looks like!
